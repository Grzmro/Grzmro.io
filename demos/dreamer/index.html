The agent that learned to play inside its own head

This program started out knowing nothing — not what a ball is, not that it was losing. After roughly two hours of training it beats the Atari bot. The interesting part is how it learned: mostly by imagining the game rather than playing it.

Why imagine instead of play

A typical game-learning program has to play the game millions of times. Every attempt costs real time: in robotics it is worn hardware, in medicine or finance a bill nobody wants to pay just so an algorithm can warm up.

This agent does something else. First it builds an internal model of the game — it learns to predict what the next frame looks like after a given move. Once that model works, the agent no longer needs the real game to practise tactics: it plays thousands of imagined rounds in its head and learns what pays off from those.

1 · Looks

Watches the screen and squeezes it into a handful of numbers describing the situation: where the ball is, where the paddles are, where it is all heading.

2 · Predicts

From that summary it predicts the next situation after any move — without consulting the real game at all.

3 · Practises in its head

Plays imagined rounds and learns tactics from them. The real game is left doing one job: gathering fresh experience.

Does it actually work better

Compared against two standard methods — PPO and DQN. All three got the same amount of practice and played exactly the same Pong. A score of −21 means losing without scoring once; 0 is a draw.

That is the whole thesis of the project in two pictures. A world model buys sample efficiency with compute. It pays off anywhere a real attempt costs more than electricity — and electricity you can just buy more of.

Evidence it is not a fluke

The strongest evidence is not in the charts above but in an experiment that tests the cause. If the agent really learns from imagined rounds, then playing more of them at the same number of real moves should make it play better. The knob that controls exactly that was set to three positions, three random seeds each, on an A100 cluster:

−20.5little imagination
−11.9medium
−6.1lots of imagination

At the lowest setting the agent learned nothing at all — all three seeds stayed at the level of flailing the paddle at random. The more imagined rounds, the better the score, even though the amount of real play was identical. That is what separates "learns from imagination" from "learns from something else, and imagination is decoration".

What this demo does not tell you

  • The match shown is 1 win out of 9 recorded attempts. The agent plays the Atari bot roughly as an equal and loses more often than it wins — I picked a winning match because it is better to watch, but the result is not typical. The eight other attempts ended 4:5 four times, 3:5 twice, then 2:5 and 1:5.
  • The imagination shown in "With imagination" mode is real — it is the model's decoded prediction from that moment of the game. But the agent does not actually stop like this while playing: imagination was what it learned from; during a match it acts on reflex.
  • The comparison against PPO and DQN rests on a single run of each — too little for a strong conclusion, which is why the 3-seed repetition is the one that runs on the cluster. The "imagination knob" experiment already has 3 seeds per setting.
  • The agent in the chart started from a finished world model and used ~110k moves in total against 100k for PPO and DQN. The version learning entirely from scratch reaches −11.9.
